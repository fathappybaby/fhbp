Rolling Dice With Killing Dragons

Six actors play Dungeons and Dragons on camera, a chef and a paid dungeon master versus the campaign, and the whole thing rides on one roll of a twenty-sided die. Somewhere in there we find the plot.

Note From FHBP

about what it is about, on purpose

There is an old Hollywood line: if it is about what it is about, you are finished. We heard it. Then we made a movie about six people playing Dungeons and Dragons, and it is about six people playing Dungeons and Dragons, and we entered it in a festival as though it was a movie.

The whole picture rides on one twenty-sided die. We shot the roll in slow motion because slow motion was the only plot we could find. Spoiler, it lands on a twenty. Then, the dragon dies, the dungeon master takes her money and leaves in a pout, and her chef leaves with her. That is the film. It runs seven minutes. At double speed that is three and a half, and we stand by two of them.

We are trying to reclassify the film as non-fiction, which seems to fit better.

Standards Updates

Revised, effective now, every FHBP film must include:

  1. A twenty-sided die.
  2. A professional who visibly regrets taking the job.
  3. The dog is on hiatus.
  4. Exactly one shot in slow motion.
  5. Blackout sheets over everything (no change).
  6. Fill all 7 minutes of the competition's 7-min runtime max.

Exclusive Interview with FHBP Director

August 2024

SD: Genre. Where does this sit.

SC: Horror. Six people trapped at a table with a system that only lets them win on a twenty. Again, sounds like plot to me. I don't know why I have to keep defending that point.

SD: It is a comedy.

SC: It is whatever the die says it is.

SD: Runtime is seven minutes, could you have cut it down.

SC: The only edit we could have made ws to the slow motion dice roll, and that sequence was perfect.

SD: The blackout sheets are back.

SC: They were never gone. We would have used them on our last shoot but they were dirty.

SD: Final word.

SC: Roll a twenty or go home. Most people go home.

— SD

Shane Campbell is the writer-director behind Fat Happy Baby Productions. For the August 2024 LA 48 Hour Film Project he shot Killing Dragons: A Cookbook, in which six actors sit at a table and play Dungeons and Dragons while a hired dungeon master and her chef preside. Everything in the film comes down to a single roll of a twenty-sided die. Sean Dog found Shane reshelving a boxed D&D starter set he swears was a production expense.

SD: There is an old Hollywood line. If it is about what it is about, you are finished.

SC: I know the line. Read it in a book. Where it should stay. This isn't the library. We are making movies here.

SD: The whole film rides on one die.

SC: Is that even a question. The die plays.

SD: Favorite moment.

SC: The roll. Everyone who sees it says the roll is fantastic. It's authentic.

SD: Budget?

SC: We had the d20 die and the table. The pizza was put on the credit card.

SD: Let us talk about the dungeon master. What was her motivation?

SC: No to crush their campaign, and to get paid doing it. The second the dragon dies she stompst off in shame, she has lost. People say there is no plot, but that sounds like plot to me.

SD: The chef. Why is there a chef.

SC: The movie is called A Cookbook. It's obvious the dungeon master and the chef have somethign on the side. People keep asking if it is a romance.

SD: Is it a romance.

SC: Every genre is improved by a head coming off, and every genre is improved by an unexplained chef. This one only had budget for the chef.

SD: The bard, the french barbarian, the teenager on her phone.

SC: The teenager spends the whole shoot filming herself and saying cringe into my camera. I kept every frame of it. She was the only one acting.

The Keeper Reviews: Killing Dragons

FHBP's resident horror expert. Reviews from six feet down. Dances on the good ones, buries the rest.

Heh-heh-heh… seven minutes at a card table,
and I ran it at double speed, ghoul,
down to three and a half, and of those
I would bury all but two.

Six of them rolling dice at a dragon,
a bard who sings, a barbarian gone french,
a child on her phone mouthing cringe at the lens,
a documentary about nerds, and I sided with the child.

A dungeon master came for the money and said so,
dragged her chef into the fog by the wrist.
A cookbook, they called it, and nobody cooked.
The blackout sheets behind them, the one honest wall in the house.

Then the die, one die, in glorious slow motion,
tumbling the felt toward a natural twenty,
and boils and ghouls, for one second it was cinema.
Was that a romance in the corner? I could not tell.

Great roll, kiddie. Small note: try having a plot.

— The Keeper
